## Customer model

### Maya, the Playgrounds campaign developer

**Today (without this CLI):** Maya keeps ScreenCloud Studio, the Playgrounds editor, a code editor, and a terminal open together. She pulls HTML/CSS/JavaScript and JSON through ad hoc requests, compares them with local files, creates a preview, and manually traces the app UUID back through Studio when she needs to understand deployment scope.

**Weekly ritual:** She revises one or more campaign Playgrounds, checks preview behavior, confirms nobody changed the remote files since her pull, and publishes reviewed changes.

**Frustration:** A file diff tells her what changed but not which spaces and app instances will display the change.

### Andre, the signage platform administrator

**Today (without this CLI):** Andre pages through spaces, app installations, instances, apps, and versions in Studio or reruns GraphQL scripts for each entity. He then joins identifiers manually and cannot reliably distinguish an inactive installation, an outdated version, a dangling instance, or divergent configuration across the fleet.

**Weekly ritual:** He inventories the organization before releases and investigates spaces whose Playgrounds content is missing, stale, inactive, or inconsistent.

**Frustration:** ScreenCloud exposes the needed records separately, but no single API response answers whether every Playgrounds deployment is ready and internally consistent.

### Lena, the content operations lead

**Today (without this CLI):** Lena starts in the template list, edits public JSON data and presentation files, previews the result, and asks a developer or administrator to verify the target. Preview workspaces, production workspaces, and their `lastModified` values are not visible as one fleet-level change queue.

**Weekly ritual:** She prepares template-based content, leaves revisions in preview, returns for stakeholder edits, and promotes approved work.

**Frustration:** She cannot quickly tell which previews are newer than production, how long they have been waiting, or whether production changed underneath them.

### Ravi, the automation engineer

**Today (without this CLI):** Ravi keeps the generated GraphQL reference, curl or Postman, and custom scripts open. He handles HTTP-200 GraphQL errors, Relay cursors, scoped management/viewer JWTs, and the undocumented Playgrounds response shapes himself.

**Weekly ritual:** He runs inventory and pre-release automation with bounded pagination, checks query cost, and validates read paths before allowing guarded mutations.

**Frustration:** Because the Playgrounds contract comes from production bundles rather than a stable published specification, ordinary endpoint success does not prove that the assumptions his automation depends on still hold.

## Phase 1.5 revision after completeness audit

The operator asked whether any stone remained unturned. The follow-up audit expanded the release topology through direct casts, associations, shares, channels, playlists, and screens; inventoried the complete official v2.103.0 schema; and live-verified the structure of the permission catalog without retaining effective grants. No mutation was performed.

### Revised release impact evidence

The release impact map remains 10/10, but its contract is stronger: it must compute spaces, channels, playlists, and screens from a bounded, freshness-stamped local graph; distinguish direct from nested placement; and mark stale or permission-incomplete results. A bounded live read-only topology query succeeded and reported `graphqlQueryCost` 1579, which is evidence for local sync and visible cost rather than repeated deep live traversal.
