## Customer model

### Energy-market analyst checking regional grid and fuel movements before the trading day

**Today (without this CLI):** The analyst opens the EIA explorer, notebooks, and saved API URLs, then retrieves several balancing-authority or fuel series and manually checks their latest timestamps, units, and frequencies. They cannot immediately tell whether a surprising move is real, stale, or caused by misaligned series.

**Weekly ritual:** Every morning, they compare the newest demand, generation, interchange, price, or inventory observations with recent local baselines.

**Frustration:** Repeating alignment and freshness checks across string-valued, route-specific datasets is slower than interpreting the market movement itself.

### Policy or climate researcher comparing states and fuels

**Today (without this CLI):** The researcher discovers routes and facets, downloads state or fuel series, and writes notebook code to parse values, align periods, retain units, and identify gaps. Similar-looking series can silently differ in frequency, sector, or unit.

**Weekly ritual:** They refresh comparative state, sector, and fuel datasets and rerun standardized comparisons for reports or models.

**Frustration:** The EIA hierarchy exposes the necessary metadata, but safe cross-series alignment must be rebuilt in each notebook.

### Energy journalist reproducing a public-data chart

**Today (without this CLI):** The journalist follows an EIA chart or article back through the web explorer, reconstructs filters and periods, and exports data into a charting tool. They struggle to preserve a compact record of exactly which route, facets, units, frequency, and source rows produced the chart.

**Weekly ritual:** They refresh recurring price, generation, demand, or fuel-mix charts and verify that revisions or missing observations have not changed the story.

**Frustration:** A chart can be visually reproducible while its underlying query, alignment choices, or revised source values remain undocumented.

### Operations or procurement analyst monitoring thresholds

**Today (without this CLI):** The analyst reruns saved scripts for prices, inventories, generation, or demand, then checks spreadsheets for threshold breaches. Rate suspensions, truncated retrievals, stale periods, and string parsing can make an apparently quiet result untrustworthy.

**Weekly ritual:** They evaluate watched series against operational thresholds and investigate deviations from recent baselines.

**Frustration:** They need a concise exception queue that includes the baseline, source observations, freshness, and unit/frequency guards—not a black-box alert.

Adversarial checks:

- **Latest grid pulse:** The market analyst runs it every morning, comfortably exceeding weekly use. It is not an electricity endpoint wrapper because it joins multiple grid measures to a local baseline and verifies freshness/alignment. Its transcendence proof is the service-specific grid pattern plus local history and agent-shaped evidence. The closest killed candidate is AI market-move explanation, which adds unverifiable causal narrative. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its Long Description is `none`.
- **Alignment-safe series comparison:** The policy researcher refreshes comparative work weekly. It is not a raw-query wrapper because it validates schemas and joins aligned observations across facets before producing a comparison. Its transcendence proof is metadata-aware local alignment. The closest killed candidate is automatic unit normalization, which hides decisions this survivor exposes or rejects. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its redirect references the surviving `spread` command exactly.
- **Guarded cross-series spread:** Market and procurement analysts evaluate recurring spreads weekly or daily. It is not a two-query wrapper because safe period/frequency/unit alignment and missing-input diagnostics create the leverage. Its transcendence proof is the guarded local join and deterministic computation. The closest killed candidate is unlimited bulk mirror, which retrieves more data without creating analytical leverage. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its redirect references surviving `state compare` exactly.
- **Explainable anomaly scan:** The named analysts investigate watched series weekly. It is not a data-query wrapper because it computes a transparent trailing baseline from historized observations and cites the contributing rows. Its transcendence proof is local time-series history and agent-shaped evidence. The closest killed candidate is energy-price forecast, which substitutes unverifiable prediction for observable deviation. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its redirect references surviving `watch run` exactly.
- **Saved watch evaluation:** The operations analyst runs the exception queue at least weekly. It is not a route wrapper because it evaluates multiple saved rules against cached observations and provenance in one deterministic pass. Its transcendence proof is the cross-series local rule engine. The closest killed candidate is interactive energy dashboard, which expands the same ritual into a persistent application. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its redirect references surviving `anomaly` exactly.
- **Source revision diff:** Journalists and researchers refreshing recurring charts can check revisions weekly before publication. It is not a query wrapper because it compares separate retrieval snapshots of the same source key. Its transcendence proof is local version history and reproducibility evidence. The closest killed candidate is series completeness certificate, whose retrieval diagnostics are less directly tied to the chart-reproduction ritual. Buildability is `hand-code`. Its redirect references surviving `anomaly` exactly.
